What this module shows

Imagine collecting a new sample of workers many times and re-estimating the same model on each sample. The fitted curves differ from sample to sample. Two things can go wrong:

  • Bias: on average over all samples, the curve misses the true relationship. Too simple a model is biased (underfitting).
  • Variance: the curve jumps around from sample to sample. Too flexible a model has high variance (overfitting).

The expected prediction error of a model on new data is bias² + variance + noise. The noise belongs to the data and is the same for every model; bias² and variance belong to the model, so the prediction error changes when the model changes. Raising complexity lowers bias but raises variance: the best model balances the two.

How to use it

  1. Each thin blue line is the model fitted on one sample. Press Play to add samples one at a time.
  2. Move the complexity slider and watch the cloud of curves: narrow but off target, or centred but spread out.
  3. The decomposition chart shows bias², variance and their sum for every complexity.
  4. Click the curves chart to look at the predictions at one value of x.

The thought experiment

An estimator is a rule that turns a sample into a fitted model. Because the sample is random, the fitted model is random too. Econometrics asks the same question about a coefficient (its sampling distribution); here we ask it about a whole prediction curve. The simulation draws many samples from the same population and refits the model on each.

Bias and variance, like darts

  • Low bias, low variance: darts close together around the bull's eye. The ideal.
  • High bias, low variance: darts close together but away from the centre. A model that is too simple, for example a straight line for a curved wage profile: every sample gives almost the same wrong line.
  • Low bias, high variance: darts centred on the bull's eye on average, but scattered. A very flexible model: right on average, but any single fit can be far off.

The noise is the part of y that no model can predict (luck, unmeasured factors). It sets a floor under the prediction error.

The decomposition at a point x₀
E\bigl[(y_0-\hat f(x_0))^2\bigr] = \underbrace{\bigl(E[\hat f(x_0)]-f(x_0)\bigr)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{E\bigl[(\hat f(x_0)-E[\hat f(x_0)])^2\bigr]}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}

The expectation is over repeated training samples and the noise of the new observation y₀. The cards average each term over the range of x.

The tradeoff

Making a model more flexible (higher polynomial degree, fewer neighbours) lets it follow the true curve more closely, so the bias falls. But it also lets it follow the noise of each particular sample, so the variance rises. The sum is U-shaped. This is the same U-shape as the test error in the Polynomial Overfitting module: there you saw one sample; here you see why.

  • More observations per sample reduce the variance, so the best complexity moves up.
  • More noise raises the variance of flexible models (and the noise floor), so the best complexity moves down.
  • The bias depends on the model family and the true curve, not on the sample size.

Econometrics and machine learning

Classical econometrics prizes unbiased estimators: under the Gauss-Markov assumptions OLS has the smallest variance among unbiased linear estimators. Machine learning cares about the total prediction error, so it accepts some bias when that buys a larger fall in variance. Ridge and lasso regression (see the Regularization module) do exactly that. Omitted-variable bias is a bias problem; adding many weak or collinear regressors is a variance problem.

Two examples
\text{k-nearest neighbours: } \operatorname{Var}\hat f(x_0) \approx \frac{\sigma^2}{k}, \qquad \text{OLS with } p \text{ regressors: } \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \operatorname{Var}\hat f(x_i) = \frac{p\,\sigma^2}{n}
Deep dive

Derivation. Write y_0 = f(x_0)+\varepsilon_0 with E\varepsilon_0=0, \operatorname{Var}\varepsilon_0=\sigma^2, independent of the training sample. Adding and subtracting \bar f(x_0)=E\hat f(x_0), the cross terms vanish because \varepsilon_0 is independent of \hat f and E[\hat f(x_0)-\bar f(x_0)]=0.

What the simulation computes. With R samples and a grid of x values, bias² is the squared gap between the average of the R fitted curves and the true curve, and the variance is the average squared distance of the R curves from their average, both averaged over the grid. Their sum plus σ² is compared with a direct simulation: the mean squared error of each fitted curve on a large fresh test sample, averaged over the R fits.

Beyond the U. For models with more parameters than observations (large neural networks, interpolating kernel methods) the test error can fall again after the interpolation point: the "double descent" curve (Belkin, Hsu, Ma and Mandal, 2019). The decomposition still holds; the variance of the minimum-norm interpolator shrinks as parameters are added.

References: Geman, Bienenstock and Doursat (1992), "Neural networks and the bias/variance dilemma", Neural Computation 4(1); Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman (2009), The Elements of Statistical Learning, §7.3; James, Witten, Hastie and Tibshirani (2021), An Introduction to Statistical Learning, §2.2; Belkin et al. (2019), PNAS 116(32).

From the simulation to real data

  • With real data you see only one sample, so bias and variance cannot be measured directly. Cross-validation estimates their sum (the expected test error) and is how the complexity is chosen in practice.
  • The bootstrap (resampling your own data with replacement) imitates "new samples": refitting on bootstrap samples and looking at the spread of predictions estimates the variance part. Random forests use exactly this idea to reduce variance.
  • When data are scarce, as with many macroeconomic series, prefer simpler models: variance dominates.
