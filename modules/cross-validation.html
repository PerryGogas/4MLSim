What this module shows

How do we choose a model (here: the degree of a polynomial for wages and experience) and report how well it predicts, honestly? With three kinds of data:

  • The training set (e.g. observations 1-16): used to fit the models and, through cross-validation, to choose among them.
  • Inside it, the validation fold: in each round of cross-validation one fold (e.g. 13-16) is left out of the fit and used to measure the error.
  • The test (hold-out) set (e.g. 17-20): locked away, used once at the very end to report the performance of the chosen model.

How to use it

  1. Press Next step and follow the numbered observations from the full data set to the final test.
  2. The box at the top of the page explains each step.
  3. Change the number of observations, the test share, the number of folds or the degree, and start again.
  4. Click any ? for help, then try the Challenges.

    Cross-validation results

    Showing the intuition only. Choose Formulas or Deep dive above to add the mathematics.

    The terms, with ten observations

    Number the observations 1 to 10.

    • Full set: 1-10.
    • Training set: 1-8. Used to fit the models and, with cross-validation, to choose the model and its settings (hyperparameters).
    • Test (hold-out) set: 9-10. Never used while choosing; touched once at the very end. "Test set" always means this set, never a fold inside cross-validation.
    • Folds (4 folds of the training set): F1 = 1-2, F2 = 3-4, F3 = 5-6, F4 = 7-8.
    • In each round of cross-validation the fold left out is the validation fold, the other three are the fitting part:
      1. fit on 3-8, validate on 1-2;
      2. fit on 1-2 and 5-8, validate on 3-4;
      3. fit on 1-4 and 7-8, validate on 5-6;
      4. fit on 1-6, validate on 7-8.
    • CV score = the average of the four validation-fold scores. The model and its settings are chosen by the CV score only.
    • The chosen model is refitted on the whole training set (1-8) and evaluated once on the test set (9-10). That result is the performance we report.
    CV score with k folds
    \text{CV}_k = \frac1k\sum_{j=1}^{k} \text{MSE}_j, \qquad \text{MSE}_j = \frac{1}{n_j}\sum_{i\in F_j}\bigl(y_i - \hat f^{(-j)}(x_i)\bigr)^2

    \hat f^{(-j)} is the model fitted without fold F_j; n_j is the number of observations in that fold.

    Why not simply use the test set to choose?

    If we tried several degrees on the test set and kept the best, the reported test error would be the best of several lucky draws: too optimistic. The test set must play the role of data that did not exist when the model was chosen. Cross-validation gives us a "test-like" error for every candidate model using only the training set.

    Why several folds and not one validation set?

    A single validation set is small and its error depends a lot on which observations happen to be in it. With k folds every training observation is used for validation exactly once, and the average of k errors is much steadier. Typical choices are k = 5 or 10; with very small samples, leave-one-out (k = number of training observations) is also used.

    After choosing: refit, then test once

    Each round of cross-validation fitted the model on only part of the training set. Once the degree is chosen, the model is fitted again on the whole training set, which uses all the information available, and only this final model is evaluated on the test set.

    Deep dive

    Bias and variance of the CV estimate. Each fold model is fitted on (k − 1)/k of the training set, so the CV score slightly overstates the error of the model fitted on the whole training set; the bias falls as k grows. Leave-one-out is almost unbiased, but its folds overlap heavily, so its errors are strongly correlated and the average can be variable; 5 or 10 folds are a good compromise (Kohavi, 1995).

    The standard error of the CV score is usually computed as the standard deviation of the fold scores divided by √k. It ignores the correlation between folds and so understates the true uncertainty (Bengio and Grandvalet, 2004). The "1-SE rule" uses it to prefer simpler models.

    Nested cross-validation. When there is too little data for a separate test set, an outer CV loop can play the role of the test set and an inner loop chooses the model in each outer training set.

    References: Stone (1974), JRSS B 36(2); Kohavi (1995), IJCAI; Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman (2009), The Elements of Statistical Learning, §7.10; James, Witten, Hastie and Tibshirani (2021), An Introduction to Statistical Learning, §5.1; Bengio and Grandvalet (2004), JMLR 5.

    From the simulation to real data

    • Split off the test (hold-out) set first, before looking at the data in any way, and do not use it until the final evaluation.
    • Everything that learns from data (scaling, choosing variables, filling missing values, tuning) belongs inside the cross-validation loop, refitted on each fitting part. Otherwise information leaks from the validation fold (see the Data Leakage module).
    • With cross-sectional data, shuffle the observations before forming folds. With time series, use walk-forward validation instead (see the Time-Series Validation module).
    • In scikit-learn: train_test_split for the test set, then cross_val_score or GridSearchCV with KFold on the training set.
    ➜4Cast Lab uses the same scheme on your own data: a final test period, and cross-validation inside the training period to choose the models.
