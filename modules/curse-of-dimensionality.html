What this module shows

Many methods rely on distances: k-nearest neighbours, k-means, DBSCAN, kernel SVMs. In two or three dimensions "near" and "far" are clear. Add more inputs (dimensions) and something strange happens: all points become about equally far from each other, and the nearest neighbour is hardly nearer than the farthest point.

This is the curse of dimensionality: the space grows so fast that any data set becomes sparse, and distance-based methods lose their footing.

How to use it

  1. Raise the number of dimensions and watch the histogram of distances bunch together.
  2. Read how large a neighbourhood must be to hold 10% of the data.
  3. Add useless inputs to a classification problem and watch KNN's test accuracy fall.

Distances lose their meaning

Spread points at random in a cube with d sides. In 2 dimensions some points are very close to a given point and some very far. As d grows, every distance is the sum of many small squared differences, one per input; by the law of large numbers these sums are all close to their average. The nearest and the farthest points end up at almost the same distance. A method that predicts from "the nearest" observations then works with neighbours that are not really near.

The space is empty

  • To capture 10% of uniformly spread data with a small cube around a point, its edge must cover 10% of each input's range in 1 dimension, 32% in 2, 79% in 10 and 98% in 100. "Local" neighbourhoods are no longer local.
  • To keep the same density of observations, the data needed grows exponentially: 10 points per input means 10 in 1 dimension, 100 in 2, 10 billion in 10.
  • Most of the volume of a high-dimensional cube lies near its surface and its corners.

Useless inputs hurt

In practice the problem appears when we add many inputs that carry no information: each adds noise to every distance. K-nearest neighbours with 2 useful inputs and 50 useless ones is close to guessing. More inputs are not always better.

Remedies

  • Feature selection: keep the inputs that matter (domain knowledge, importance measures, lasso).
  • Dimension reduction: summarize many correlated inputs with a few components (PCA).
  • More data, and models that do not rely on distances in all inputs at once (trees and their ensembles pick inputs one at a time; regularized regressions shrink useless coefficients).
  • Real data are rarely spread evenly: they usually lie near a lower-dimensional structure, which is why many methods still work with many inputs.

Deep dive

Concentration of distances. For points uniform in [0, 1]d, the squared distance between two points has mean d/6 and standard deviation of order √d, so the distance is about √(d/6) with a spread that does not grow: relative to its size, the spread shrinks like 1/√d.

The unit ball. The volume of the ball of radius 1/2 inside the unit cube is about 0.785 in 2 dimensions, 0.0025 in 10 and practically 0 in 20: almost all of the cube is in its corners.

References: Bellman (1961), Adaptive Control Processes; Beyer, Goldstein, Ramakrishnan and Shaft (1999), "When is nearest neighbor meaningful?", ICDT; Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman (2009), The Elements of Statistical Learning, §2.5.

From the simulation to real data

  • With many indicators (hundreds of macro series, text features, many firm ratios), prefer feature selection, regularization, PCA or tree ensembles to raw KNN or k-means.
