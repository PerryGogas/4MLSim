What this module shows

Data leakage happens when information that will not be available when the model is really used finds its way into training or evaluation. The model then looks better in cross-validation, and often also on the test (hold-out) set, than it really is.

The key message: leakage makes a model look better, but on new, unknown data it performs no better than the correct model, and sometimes worse.

Three common cases from economics and finance: a variable known only after the outcome, choosing predictors with all the data, and the same firm in training and validation. Time series have their own module: Time-Series Validation.

What leakage is

A model is built to be used at a certain moment, with the information available at that moment: a bank decides on a loan at application, a forecaster forecasts with the data published so far, an analyst predicts for firms not yet in the data. Leakage is any way in which information that will not be available at that moment enters the training or the evaluation of the model. Cross-validation, and often the test (hold-out) set too, then measure the wrong thing and report a performance that is too good. Only the true score on new data reveals it.

The key message. Leakage makes a model look better than it is, but it does not make it better. On new, unknown data the leaky model performs no better than the correct model, and sometimes worse. The only thing the leak changes is the promise: the reported scores are too good, so decisions are taken on a model that will disappoint.

For every case ask the same question: which data did each decision see? Every decision that looks at data (estimating coefficients, choosing predictors, scaling, removing outliers) is part of the model. Data used to make a decision can no longer give an honest check of it.

1. A variable known too late

  • What the analyst does: uses every column of the historical data, including late-payment fees, to predict loan default.
  • What goes wrong: the fees are charged after the borrower misses payments, so they almost reveal the outcome. They are in the validation folds and in the test set too, because these come from the same historical file. New applicants have no fees yet.
  • On new data: the model with the fees cannot even be used, because new applicants have no fees. At the default settings it promised an AUC of 0.996; the bank gets the model without fees, which delivers 0.764 on new applicants.
  • Correct way: for every variable ask would I know this at the moment of prediction? Keep only those. Other examples: the number of collection calls, a "closed account" flag, a firm's delisting date in a bankruptcy model.

2. Choosing predictors with all the data

One firm, monthly data: training set months 1-48 (4 folds: 1-12, 13-24, 25-36, 37-48), test (hold-out) set months 49-60. The analyst has 500 candidate predictors and keeps the 8 most correlated with the stock return.

  • Choose on all data (leak). The correlations are computed with all 60 months, training + test. Among many useless predictors some correlate with the return by pure chance in these months, and those are the ones picked, partly because they fit months 37-48 (a validation fold) and months 49-60 (the test set). Cross-validation and the test then measure a fit that the choice itself created: both look too good.
  • Choose on the training set, then cross-validate (partial leak). The test set is locked away first, the 8 predictors are picked once with months 1-48, and then 4-fold cross-validation is run. In each round the validation fold, e.g. months 37-48, was part of the months used to choose, so cross-validation is still too optimistic. The test set is honest, because months 49-60 took no part in the choice. Warning sign: cross-validation promises much more than the test delivers.
  • Choose inside each fold (correct). In each round the 8 predictors are picked with the 3 fitting folds only (e.g. months 1-36) and validated on the fourth (37-48). The rounds may pick different predictors; that is normal. For the final model the predictors are picked once more with months 1-48, and the model is tested once on months 49-60. Every score is measured on months that took no part in the choice.

The rule: every step that looks at the data to make a decision (choosing predictors, choosing lags, removing outliers, scaling) is repeated inside each round of cross-validation with the fitting part only, and never sees the test set. A scikit-learn Pipeline does this automatically. (With scaling the effect is usually small; with predictor selection among many candidates it can be large.)

Out-of-sample R². R² = 1 − (sum of squared forecast errors of the model) / (sum of squared errors when forecasting every month with the average return of the training months). 0 means no better than the average; negative means worse than the average. In the simulation the true predictability is much larger than for real stock returns, so that the leak is easy to see.

3. The same firm on both sides

  • What the analyst does: splits firm-years at random into folds and test set, in a panel of firms observed over several years.
  • What goes wrong: every firm has years in the training part and years in the validation fold or the test set. The model recognises the firm from characteristics that never change (size, founding year, region) and copies its usual level. For a new firm this shortcut does not exist.
  • Correct way: split by firm (grouped folds), when the model is built for new firms. The same holds for customers, households, banks or countries.

Time series

Time series can leak too: shuffled folds let the model train on months after the month it forecasts, and walking forward without a gap uses examples whose target month is not yet known. See the Time-Series Validation module.

How to avoid leakage

  • Write down, for every variable, when it becomes known; keep only variables known at prediction time.
  • Split off the test (hold-out) set first; do every step that learns from data inside the cross-validation loop.
  • Split by group when observations belong to the same firm, customer, household or country.
  • For time series, walk forward and leave a gap for forecasts more than one month ahead (Time-Series Validation module).
  • Be suspicious of results that look too good: an AUC near 1 for credit default, or a large R² for monthly stock returns, is almost always leakage.
Deep dive

Why the test set often does not catch it. The test set is drawn from the same data and processed by the same pipeline. If the leaky variable is in the data, it is in the test set too; if selection used all observations, it also used the test set. Only data generated after the model is frozen, under the conditions of real use, is safe.

Selection bias. Choosing m of p candidate predictors by their sample correlation selects, among the useless ones, those whose sample correlation is largest by chance. Evaluating the selected model on the same observations measures this chance correlation as if it were real (Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman, 2009, §7.10.2: "the wrong and the right way to do cross-validation"). In empirical finance the same problem appears as data snooping over hundreds of candidate factors (Harvey, Liu and Zhu, 2016).

Grouped cross-validation. GroupKFold keeps all observations of a unit in the same fold. For overlapping time-series targets, López de Prado (2018) proposes purging training examples whose targets overlap the validation period and an embargo after it (see the Time-Series Validation module).

References: Kaufman, Rosset, Perlich and Stitelman (2012), "Leakage in data mining: formulation, detection, and avoidance", ACM TKDD 6(4); Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman (2009), The Elements of Statistical Learning, §7.10; Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016), "...and the cross-section of expected returns", Review of Financial Studies 29(1); Kapoor and Narayanan (2023), "Leakage and the reproducibility crisis in machine-learning-based science", Patterns 4(9); López de Prado (2018), Advances in Financial Machine Learning, ch. 7.

From the simulation to real data

  • Credit scoring, churn and fraud: check the date of every variable against the decision date.
  • Firm, bank and country panels: use grouped folds when the model must work for new units, and time-ordered splits when it must work for future years.
  • Macroeconomic forecasting: use the data published at each forecast date (real-time data), not today's revised series, and leave a gap of h months for forecasts h months ahead.
  • Asset returns: an out-of-sample R² of even 1-2% per month is economically large; results far above that deserve a search for leakage.
