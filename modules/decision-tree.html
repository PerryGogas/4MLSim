What this module shows

A decision tree decides in steps, like we do every day ("Will I accept the job? Is the salary above 40,000? Is the commute under 30 minutes?"). Each split asks one yes/no question about one input.

The tree is grown split by split: at each step it tries every possible question and keeps the one that makes the groups purest (lowest Gini impurity or entropy; for numbers, the smallest squared error). It works for classification and for regression.

How to use it

  1. Choose classification or regression, and the data.
  2. Press Reset, then Step: one split at a time. The next node to split is outlined in orange.
  3. Read "Choosing the question": the impurity for every possible threshold.
  4. Raise the maximum depth and compare training and test results.

Decisions in steps

Decision trees are easy to understand: we use the same logic every day. A tree starts with all the training observations at the top (the root). It asks one yes/no question about one input, for example "Is the diameter at most 3 cm?", and sends the observations left (yes) or right (no). Each group is split again, until the groups are pure or the tree reaches its maximum depth. The final groups are the leaves; a new observation follows the questions down to a leaf and gets its prediction: the most common class in the leaf (classification) or the average value (regression).

Choosing the question: impurity

For every input and every possible threshold (halfway between two neighbouring values), the tree computes how mixed the two resulting groups would be, and keeps the question with the purest groups. Gini impurity measures how "messy" a group is: imagine a box of balls of different colours; pick two at random, with replacement. Gini is the probability that they have different colours. A box of one colour has Gini 0; half red and half blue gives 0.5. Entropy is a similar measure from information theory: 0 for a pure group, 1 bit for a 50/50 mix of two classes. Both usually choose the same questions.

Impurity of a group with class shares pk, and the split score
\text{Gini} = 1 - \sum_k p_k^2, \qquad \text{Entropy} = -\sum_k p_k \log_2 p_k
\text{impurity after the split} = \frac{n_L}{n}\, I_L + \frac{n_R}{n}\, I_R \qquad \text{(choose the smallest)}

Regression trees use the squared error: I = average of (y − group mean)². The prediction of a leaf is the group mean.

How deep?

  • A deep tree keeps splitting until almost every leaf is pure: it fits the training set very well, but the last splits follow noise. The test set shows the overfitting.
  • A shallow tree (depth 1 or 2) may miss real patterns (underfitting).
  • Depth and the minimum number of observations per leaf are chosen by cross-validation on the training set; pruning cuts back splits that do not help.

Strengths and weaknesses

  • Strengths: simple to build, use and interpret; no scaling needed; handles numbers and categories; the rules can be read and audited (credit, medicine, the electricity theft example).
  • Weaknesses: unstable (a small change in the data can give a very different tree) and inaccurate on their own. Random forests and boosting combine many trees to fix this.
  • The boundaries are always made of straight lines parallel to the axes: boxes.
Deep dive

CART. The algorithm here is CART (Breiman, Friedman, Olshen and Stone, 1984), as in scikit-learn: binary splits, thresholds at midpoints, greedy choice of the best split at each node. Greedy means the best question now, not the best tree overall: finding the optimal tree is computationally infeasible.

Information gain. With entropy, the drop in impurity is the information gain of the question. Gini and entropy differ in only a few percent of splits in practice.

Cost-complexity pruning. Grow a large tree, then remove the splits whose reduction in error is smallest relative to the number of leaves they add, choosing the penalty α by cross-validation.

References: Breiman, Friedman, Olshen and Stone (1984), Classification and Regression Trees; Quinlan (1986), "Induction of decision trees", Machine Learning 1; Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman (2009), The Elements of Statistical Learning, §9.2.

From the simulation to real data

  • Credit and lending decisions, fraud and electricity theft detection, medical triage: wherever the rules must be read and explained, a small tree is attractive.
  • Choose the depth (or the minimum leaf size) by cross-validation on the training set, then test once.
  • For accuracy, use many trees: random forests and gradient boosting (next modules).
