What this module shows

A bank describes each customer by age (22 to 70 years) and annual income (about 12,000 to 120,000 euros). To a computer these are just numbers. A difference of 1,000 euros of income counts a thousand times more than a difference of 1 year of age, only because income is measured in euros.

Algorithms that use distances (k-nearest neighbours, clustering, SVM) or gradient descent (neural networks, logistic regression) are then dominated by the variable with the largest numbers. Scaling puts the variables on comparable scales.

How to use it

  1. Nearest neighbours: see which customers count as "closest" to a chosen customer, with and without scaling. Click the left chart to pick another customer.
  2. Gradient descent: see how many steps training needs with raw and with scaled variables.
  3. Switch between the scaling methods, and add a few millionaires to see which method copes with outliers.

Units are arbitrary, numbers are not

Income measured in euros or in thousands of euros is the same information, but the numbers differ by a factor of 1,000. Econometric results are not affected: an OLS coefficient simply rescales (a coefficient per euro becomes a coefficient per thousand euros) and t-statistics, p-values and predictions stay the same. Many machine learning methods are not like that: they compare the raw numbers across variables.

Where scaling matters

  • Distances: k-nearest neighbours, k-means and hierarchical clustering, DBSCAN, support vector machines with RBF kernels and PCA all compute distances or variances across variables. With raw units, the distance between two customers is essentially their income difference; age is ignored.
  • Gradient descent: with variables on very different scales the loss surface is a long, narrow valley. A step size small enough for the steep direction is far too small for the flat one, so training crawls (see the Gradient Descent module).
  • Penalties: ridge, lasso and elastic net penalize the size of the coefficients, and a coefficient's size depends on the units of its variable. Without scaling the penalty treats variables unequally (see the Regularization module).
  • Not needed: decision trees, random forests and gradient boosting split one variable at a time at a threshold, so any monotonic rescaling gives the same tree. OLS itself also does not need it.

Four ways to scale

Scaling formulas (parameters from the training data)
\text{Min-max: } x' = \frac{x-\min(x)}{\max(x)-\min(x)} \qquad \text{Standardize: } x' = \frac{x-\bar x}{s_x} \qquad \text{Robust: } x' = \frac{x-\operatorname{median}(x)}{\operatorname{IQR}(x)}
  • Min-max maps the training data to [0, 1]. Easy to read, but a single extreme value (one millionaire) defines the maximum and squeezes everyone else into a tiny interval.
  • Standardization (z-scores) gives mean 0 and standard deviation 1. The default choice; outliers still inflate the standard deviation somewhat.
  • Robust scaling uses the median and the interquartile range, which ignore the extremes. Preferred when the data contain outliers, as income and wealth data usually do.
  • A log transform before scaling is often sensible for skewed money variables (income, sales, firm size).

Fit the scaler on the training data only

The mean, standard deviation, minimum or median must be computed from the training data and then applied, unchanged, to the test data and to any new data. Computing them on all the data lets information from the test set leak into training (see the Data Leakage module). The same parameters must be stored and used when the model is deployed.

Deep dive

Distances. The Euclidean distance d(a,b)^2 = \sum_j (a_j-b_j)^2 weighs each variable by its squared units. Scaling variable j by 1/s_j is the same as using a weighted distance with weights 1/s_j^2; standardizing gives the "standardized Euclidean" distance. The Mahalanobis distance (a-b)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(a-b) goes further and also removes correlation between variables.

Gradient descent. For least squares the Hessian is X^\top X/n; with the best fixed step the error shrinks by (\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1) per iteration, where \kappa=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min} is the condition number. Raw income in euros next to age in years gives κ in the billions; standardizing brings it close to 1 + |correlation| over 1 − |correlation|. The number of iterations needed grows roughly in proportion to κ.

Equivariance. OLS predictions are invariant to rescaling the regressors; ridge and lasso predictions are not, which is why the penalty is defined on standardized variables. Trees are invariant to any strictly increasing transformation of a variable.

From the simulation to real data

  • Credit scoring, customer segmentation and fraud detection mix money amounts, ages, counts and ratios: scale them before using k-nearest neighbours, clustering, SVMs or neural networks.
  • Income, wealth, sales and firm size are heavily skewed: consider a log transform and robust scaling.
  • Dummy variables (0/1) are usually left as they are, or scaled together with the rest; be consistent.
  • In scikit-learn, put the scaler and the model in one Pipeline so that cross-validation refits the scaler inside each fold automatically.
