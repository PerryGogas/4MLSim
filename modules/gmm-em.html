What this module shows

Markets alternate between calm and turbulent periods, but no one labels the days. A Gaussian mixture assumes the data come from a few bell-shaped groups (components), each with its own center, spread and tilt, and finds them.

It is soft clustering: every observation gets a probability of belonging to each component. The parameters are found with the EM algorithm: an E-step (probabilities) and an M-step (update the components), repeated.

How to use it

  1. Press Step to see one E-step and one M-step at a time; watch the ellipses move.
  2. Click a day to see its probabilities of each regime.
  3. Choose the number of components with BIC, and compare with k-means on the stretched groups.

A mixture of bell curves

A Gaussian mixture says: each observation was produced by one of K components, chosen with probabilities w1, ..., wK (the weights); component k is a two-dimensional bell curve with its own mean (center) and covariance (spread and tilt, drawn as an ellipse). Which component produced which observation is unknown: that is what we want to find.

Example: daily stock and bond returns. On calm days both move little; on turbulent days stocks swing widely and bonds tend to rise when stocks fall (flight to safety). A mixture with K = 2 recovers the two regimes, their shares, volatilities and correlations, without any labels.

The EM algorithm

  1. Start with some components (here from k-means, as scikit-learn does, or at random).
  2. E-step (expectation): for every observation, compute the probability that each component produced it, with Bayes' rule: weight × density, divided by the sum over components. These are the responsibilities.
  3. M-step (maximization): update each component using all observations, weighted by their responsibilities: new weight = average responsibility, new mean = weighted mean, new covariance = weighted covariance.
  4. Repeat until the log-likelihood stops rising.

Each round raises the log-likelihood (or keeps it), so EM always converges, but possibly to a local maximum that depends on the start.

Mixtures versus k-means

  • K-means gives hard assignments and round clusters of similar size. A mixture gives probabilities and ellipses that can stretch and tilt, with different sizes.
  • K-means is the special case of a mixture with equal, round, shrinking components.

How many components? BIC

More components always raise the likelihood. The Bayesian information criterion charges a penalty for every parameter: choose the K with the lowest BIC.

The model and EM
p(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k\, \mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu_k, \Sigma_k), \qquad \text{E-step: } r_{ik} = \frac{w_k\, \mathcal{N}(x_i \mid \mu_k, \Sigma_k)}{\sum_{j} w_j\, \mathcal{N}(x_i \mid \mu_j, \Sigma_j)}
\text{M-step: } N_k = \sum_i r_{ik}, \quad w_k = \frac{N_k}{n}, \quad \mu_k = \frac{1}{N_k} \sum_i r_{ik} x_i, \quad \Sigma_k = \frac{1}{N_k} \sum_i r_{ik} (x_i - \mu_k)(x_i - \mu_k)^\top
\log L = \sum_i \log p(x_i), \qquad \text{BIC} = -2 \log L + q \log n, \quad q = 6K - 1 \text{ (2 inputs, full covariances)}
Deep dive

Why EM works. For any responsibilities, the log-likelihood is at least a lower bound built from them; the E-step makes the bound touch the log-likelihood, the M-step maximizes the bound. Hence the log-likelihood never falls.

Degenerate solutions. A component can shrink onto a single point and the likelihood goes to infinity. Software adds a tiny constant to the covariance diagonals (scikit-learn: reg_covar = 1e-6, also used here).

Regime switching. In economics the hidden regime usually persists from one period to the next. Hamilton's Markov-switching model adds transition probabilities between regimes and is estimated with the same E and M logic over time.

References: Dempster, Laird and Rubin (1977), "Maximum likelihood from incomplete data via the EM algorithm", JRSS B; Hamilton (1989), "A new approach to the economic analysis of nonstationary time series and the business cycle", Econometrica; McLachlan and Peel (2000), Finite Mixture Models.
