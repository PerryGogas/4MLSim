What this module shows

Hierarchical clustering does not need the number of clusters in advance. It starts with every observation as its own cluster and, step by step, merges the two closest clusters, until only one is left.

The history of the merges is drawn as a tree, the dendrogram. Cut the tree at some height and you get the clusters. How "closest" is measured (the linkage) changes the result.

How to use it

  1. Press Step and follow the merges on the map and in the dendrogram.
  2. Run to the end, then move the cut (or click on the dendrogram) to choose the number of clusters.
  3. Compare the four linkages, especially on the moons and the stretched groups.

Bottom-up merging

Agglomerative (bottom-up) hierarchical clustering:

  1. Start with n clusters, one per observation.
  2. Find the two clusters that are closest and merge them.
  3. Repeat until one cluster contains everything (n − 1 merges).

No number of clusters is needed in advance: the whole hierarchy is computed once, and the number of clusters is chosen afterwards by cutting the tree.

Linkage: the distance between two clusters

  • Single: the distance of the two closest members. Follows long, curved shapes, but can chain different groups together through a few points in between.
  • Complete: the distance of the two farthest members. Gives compact clusters of similar diameter.
  • Average: the average of all distances between members of the two clusters. A compromise.
  • Ward: merge the pair whose merging increases the within-cluster sum of squares the least. Gives round clusters of similar size, close to k-means. The most common choice for economic and business data.

Reading the dendrogram

The leaves at the bottom are the observations. Each horizontal bar is a merge, drawn at the height of the distance between the two clusters. Cutting the tree with a horizontal line gives the clusters: every branch hanging below the line is one cluster. A large jump in merge height means two well-separated groups were forced together: cut just below it.

Linkage formulas (Euclidean distance d)
\text{single: } \min_{a \in A, b \in B} d(a, b), \quad \text{complete: } \max_{a \in A, b \in B} d(a, b), \quad \text{average: } \frac{1}{|A||B|} \sum_{a \in A} \sum_{b \in B} d(a, b)
\text{Ward (as SciPy): } d(A, B) = \sqrt{\frac{2 |A| |B|}{|A| + |B|}}\; \lVert \bar x_A - \bar x_B \rVert

With this scaling, half the squared Ward height is the increase in the within-cluster sum of squares caused by the merge.

Deep dive

Lance-Williams update. After merging A and B into C, the distance from C to any other cluster Q is computed from d(A, Q), d(B, Q) and d(A, B) alone, so the n × n distance matrix is updated without revisiting the observations. This is how the module (and SciPy) computes all four linkages.

Cost. The distance matrix needs memory of order n², and the simple algorithm time of order n³; hierarchical clustering suits hundreds or a few thousand observations, k-means millions.

Cophenetic correlation. The correlation between the original distances and the dendrogram heights at which pairs first join measures how faithfully the tree represents the data.

References: Ward (1963), "Hierarchical grouping to optimize an objective function", JASA; Lance and Williams (1967), The Computer Journal; Kaufman and Rousseeuw (1990), Finding Groups in Data.

From the simulation to real data

  • Grouping countries by macro indicators, banks by their ratios, or products by their sales patterns, when the number of groups is not known and a tree of similarities is itself useful.
  • Standardize the inputs, use Ward linkage as a default, and look for the large jumps in merge height. In Python: scipy.cluster.hierarchy.linkage(X, "ward") and dendrogram.
