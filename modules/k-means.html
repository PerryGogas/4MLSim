What this module shows

A supermarket knows how much each customer spends and how often they visit, but nobody has told it which types of customers it has. Clustering finds groups in data without labels: this is unsupervised learning.

K-means puts k centers in the data and repeats two steps: assign every observation to the nearest center, then move every center to the mean of its observations. It stops when nothing changes.

How to use it

  1. Press Step and watch the two steps one at a time: assign, then move the centers.
  2. Read the elbow and silhouette charts to choose k.

Clustering: groups without labels

In classification every observation comes with its class (failed or healthy bank). In clustering there are no labels: the algorithm must discover groups of similar observations on its own. Typical uses: customer segments for marketing, types of firms or countries, market regimes, grouping of stocks.

The k-means algorithm

  1. Choose the number of clusters k and k starting centers.
  2. Assign every observation to the nearest center (the plane is split into k regions with straight borders).
  3. Update: move every center to the mean (the centroid) of its observations.
  4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until no observation changes cluster.

Each step lowers (or keeps) the within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS): the total squared distance of the observations from their centers. So k-means always stops, but possibly at a poor solution that depends on the start.

Good starts: k-means++

Random starting points can fall in the same group. k-means++ picks the first center at random and every next center far from the ones already chosen (with probability proportional to the squared distance). In practice k-means is also run from several starts and the solution with the lowest WCSS is kept (here: 5 starts in the elbow chart).

How many clusters?

  • Elbow: WCSS always falls as k grows (with k = n it is 0). Look for the k after which it falls only slowly.
  • Silhouette: for each observation, compare a = its mean distance to its own cluster with b = its mean distance to the nearest other cluster. Close to 1: well inside its cluster; near 0: on a border; negative: probably in the wrong cluster. Choose the k with the highest mean silhouette.
  • Above all, the clusters must make sense for the question (can marketing use 3 segments, or 8?).

Scaling and limits

K-means uses distances, so an input measured in large numbers (amount in €) dominates one measured in small numbers (hour of the day): standardize the inputs first (see the Feature Scaling module). K-means also assumes round groups of similar size: it cuts moons and stretched groups with straight lines. DBSCAN and Gaussian mixtures handle such shapes.

The objective and the silhouette
\text{WCSS} = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \sum_{i \in C_j} \lVert x_i - \mu_j \rVert^2, \qquad \mu_j = \frac{1}{|C_j|} \sum_{i \in C_j} x_i
s_i = \frac{b_i - a_i}{\max(a_i, b_i)}, \qquad \bar s = \frac{1}{n} \sum_i s_i

ai: mean distance of observation i to the other members of its cluster; bi: the smallest mean distance to the members of another cluster.

Deep dive

Why it converges. The assign step can only lower WCSS (each point goes to its nearest center), and the mean is the point with the smallest sum of squared distances to a group, so the update step can only lower it too. With finitely many ways to split n points, the algorithm stops at a local minimum. Finding the global minimum is NP-hard.

k-means++ gives a solution whose expected WCSS is within a factor of order log k of the best one, before any iteration.

Link to Gaussian mixtures. K-means is the limit of a Gaussian mixture with equal, round covariances that shrink to zero: soft assignments become hard ones.

References: Lloyd (1982), "Least squares quantization in PCM", IEEE Trans. Information Theory; Arthur and Vassilvitskii (2007), "k-means++: the advantages of careful seeding", SODA; Rousseeuw (1987), "Silhouettes", J. Computational and Applied Mathematics.
