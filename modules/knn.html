What this module shows

K-nearest neighbours (KNN) classifies a new observation by a vote of the k closest training observations. There is no equation and no estimation: the training data themselves are the model.

Three choices matter: the number of neighbours k, how distance is measured, and whether the inputs are standardized so that both count equally.

How to use it

  1. Click anywhere on the chart to place a new observation (the star) and see its k nearest neighbours vote.
  2. Move k and watch the boundary change from jagged to smooth.
  3. Choose k by 4-fold cross-validation on the training set, then read the test accuracy.
  4. Click any ? for help, then try the Challenges.

How KNN classifies

  1. Measure the distance from the new observation to every training observation.
  2. Rank the distances from shortest to longest (ascending order).
  3. The k first neighbours vote; the class with most votes wins. (Usually for classification, but it also works for regression: the forecast is the average of the k neighbours.)

KNN learns nothing in advance: it keeps the training data and does all the work when a new observation arrives. That is why it is called a "lazy" learner.

Choosing k

  • k = 1: each point copies its single nearest neighbour. Every training observation is its own nearest neighbour, so the training accuracy is 100%, but the boundary follows every noisy point: overfitting.
  • Very large k: the vote is taken over almost all the data, so every point gets the majority class: underfitting.
  • For 2 classes choose an odd k, so there are no ties. (Ties here go to the class of the closest neighbour among the tied classes.)
  • k is chosen by 4-fold cross-validation on the training set: for each k, train on 3 folds, validate on the fourth, average the accuracy. The test set is used once, at the end, for the chosen k.

Distance and scaling

Distances add up differences in the inputs. If one input is measured in large numbers (a PMI around 50) and the other in small numbers (a change in unemployment of about 1 point), the large one dominates the distance and the small one is almost ignored. Standardizing each input (subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation of the training set) makes both count equally. See the Feature Scaling module.

Distances between observations a and b
\text{Euclidean: } d(a,b) = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_j (a_j - b_j)^2}, \qquad \text{Manhattan: } d(a,b) = \textstyle\sum_j |a_j - b_j|
\text{standardized input: } z_j = \frac{x_j - \bar x_j}{s_j} \quad (\bar x_j,\ s_j \text{ from the training set})

On the chart, the neighbourhood of the star is a circle (Euclidean) or a diamond (Manhattan) in standardized units; in the original units it is stretched into an ellipse.

Advantages and disadvantages

  • Advantages: simple and intuitive; no assumptions about the shape of the boundary; easy to add new data.
  • Disadvantages: slow on large data sets (it computes the distance to every training observation); sensitive to the scale of the inputs and to useless inputs; weak with many inputs (distances become similar, the curse of dimensionality).
Deep dive

Weighted KNN. Closer neighbours can get larger votes, e.g. weights 1 / d. This also removes most ties.

Consistency. As the sample grows, with k → ∞ and k / n → 0, the KNN error tends to the lowest possible (Bayes) error (Stone, 1977). With k = 1 the error is at most twice the Bayes error in the limit (Cover and Hart, 1967).

Curse of dimensionality. With many inputs, the nearest and the farthest neighbours are at almost the same distance, so "nearest" loses its meaning. Feature selection or dimension reduction (PCA) helps.

References: Cover and Hart (1967), "Nearest neighbor pattern classification", IEEE Transactions on Information Theory 13(1); Stone (1977), "Consistent nonparametric regression", Annals of Statistics 5(4); Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman (2009), The Elements of Statistical Learning, ch. 13.

From the simulation to real data

  • Always standardize the inputs (with the training set's mean and standard deviation) unless they are already in the same units.
  • Choose k by cross-validation on the training set; report the test accuracy once.
  • Keep the number of inputs small and relevant: useless inputs add noise to every distance.
  • Applications in economics and finance: classifying the phase of the business cycle from indicators, credit decisions by similarity to past borrowers, recommendations ("customers like you bought..."), and analogue forecasting (find past periods that look like today).
