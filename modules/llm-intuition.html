What this module shows

A large language model (LLM) such as ChatGPT or Claude writes by repeating one simple step: given the text so far, it computes a probability for every possible next word (token), picks one, and adds it to the text.

This module builds a tiny language model from 64 sentences about economics, so you can see every number: the next-word probabilities, how temperature changes them, word embeddings (words as points in space) and attention (which earlier words matter for each word).

How to use it

  1. Choose or type a prompt and read the next-word probabilities.
  2. Change the temperature and generate text: low is safe and repetitive, high is creative and odd.
  3. Look at the word map: which words sit together, and why?

Next-word prediction

A language model assigns a probability to every word that could come next. Writing a text is just repeating: compute the probabilities, choose a word, append it, repeat. Everything an LLM "knows" is stored in how it computes these probabilities.

The tiny model here counts in its 64 training sentences how often each word followed the previous two words (a trigram model). If it never saw the last two words together, it uses only the last word (bigram), and if even that is new, the overall word frequencies. Real LLMs replace counting with a neural network (a transformer) with billions of weights, trained on trillions of words, that looks at thousands of previous tokens at once.

Temperature and top-k

  • Temperature reshapes the probabilities before a word is drawn. Below 1 it sharpens them (the likely words become more likely; near 0 the model always picks the top word). Above 1 it flattens them (rare words get a chance: more varied, but less sensible text).
  • Top-k keeps only the k most likely words and draws among them, which cuts off very unlikely words.

Embeddings: words as points

A model needs numbers, not words. Each word becomes a vector (a list of numbers), its embedding, so that words used in similar contexts get similar vectors. "Words are known by the company they keep": inflation and prices, rise and fall, loans and deposits. Here the vectors come from counting which words appear within two places of each other, then compressing with PCA; real models learn them during training.

Attention

To predict the next word, a transformer lets every word look back at all earlier words and decide how much each one matters: attention. In "house prices fall when interest rates rise", the word "rise" should attend to "rates". Each attention weight is a softmax of a similarity score between two words' vectors. The heat map here uses the toy embeddings as both queries and keys; a real model learns separate query and key projections and uses dozens of attention heads in many layers.

The formulas
P(w_t = w \mid w_{t-2}, w_{t-1}) = \frac{\text{count}(w_{t-2}\, w_{t-1}\, w)}{\text{count}(w_{t-2}\, w_{t-1})}, \qquad p_i(T) = \frac{p_i^{1/T}}{\sum_j p_j^{1/T}} = \frac{e^{z_i / T}}{\sum_j e^{z_j / T}}
\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \operatorname{softmax}\Bigl(\frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d}}\Bigr) V, \qquad \text{PPMI}(a, b) = \max\Bigl(0, \log \frac{P(a, b)}{P(a)\, P(b)}\Bigr)

Deep dive

Training objective. An LLM is trained to minimize the cross-entropy (log loss) of the actual next token: the same log loss as in logistic regression, over a vocabulary of about 100,000 tokens. Perplexity = exp(average log loss).

Tokens. Real models split text into sub-word tokens (byte-pair encoding), so new words are built from known pieces; this toy uses whole words.

After pre-training. Assistants are further trained to follow instructions and to be helpful and safe (fine-tuning, reinforcement learning from human feedback; see the Q-Learning module for the idea of learning from rewards).
