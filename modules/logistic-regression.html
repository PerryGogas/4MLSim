What this module shows

Logistic regression (the logit) predicts a probability between 0 and 1, for example that a bank fails. It turns a straight-line score b0 + b1 × x into a probability with the S-shaped sigmoid curve.

The coefficients are estimated by maximum likelihood: the values that make the observed outcomes most probable. A threshold T then turns each probability into a class.

Why not an ordinary regression line?

The outcome is 0 or 1 (healthy or failed bank). A straight line fitted to 0s and 1s (the linear probability model) gives "probabilities" below 0 or above 1 for extreme values of x. Logistic regression bends the line into an S between 0 and 1.

From a score to a probability

First a straight-line score: score = b0 + b1 × x. Then the sigmoid turns the score into a probability: a score of 0 gives 0.5, large positive scores approach 1, large negative scores approach 0. If b1 > 0 the probability rises with x (more debt, more failures); if b1 < 0 it falls (a higher term spread, fewer recessions).

The model
P(y_i = 1 \mid x_i) = p_i = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(b_0 + b_1 x_i)}}, \qquad \ln\frac{p_i}{1 - p_i} = b_0 + b_1 x_i

The left side of the second equation is the log-odds: the logarithm of the odds p / (1 − p). It is linear in x.

Reading the coefficients: odds ratios

The odds are p / (1 − p): odds of 1 mean "1 to 1" (p = 0.5), odds of 3 mean p = 0.75. When x rises by one unit, the odds are multiplied by eb1, the odds ratio. With eb1 = 2, each extra unit of debt-to-equity doubles the odds of failure. The effect on the probability itself is largest where p is near 0.5.

Estimation: maximum likelihood

For given coefficients, every observation gets a probability. The likelihood is the probability of seeing exactly the outcomes in the data: the product of pi for the 1s and of (1 − pi) for the 0s. Maximum likelihood picks the coefficients that make this as large as possible. We work with its logarithm, the log-likelihood (a sum, always negative; closer to 0 is better).

There is no formula for the maximum, so it is found step by step. Newton's method, used by statsmodels and most econometric software, starts at b0 = b1 = 0 (every probability 0.5), looks at the slope and the curvature of the log-likelihood, and jumps to the top of the parabola that fits there. It reaches the maximum in a handful of steps. (The Gradient Descent module solves the same problem with small downhill steps.)

Log-likelihood and Newton step
\ell(b) = \sum_i \bigl[\, y_i \ln p_i + (1 - y_i) \ln(1 - p_i) \,\bigr]
g = X^\top (y - p), \qquad H = X^\top W X,\; W = \mathrm{diag}\bigl(p_i (1 - p_i)\bigr), \qquad b_{\text{new}} = b + H^{-1} g

Standard errors are the square roots of the diagonal of H−1 at the maximum. McFadden's pseudo R² = 1 − ℓ(b) / ℓ0, where ℓ0 is the log-likelihood with the constant only.

From probabilities to classes: the threshold

An observation is classified as 1 when its probability is at least the threshold T (by default 0.5). With one input this is the same as a cutoff on x: the value where the S-curve crosses T. Lowering T moves the cutoff and finds more 1s (higher sensitivity) but also raises false alarms. The Confusion Matrix and ROC modules take this further.

Econometrics and machine learning

  • Econometrics reads the coefficient table: is b1 significantly different from 0 (p-value), how large is the odds ratio?
  • Machine learning asks how well the model classifies new data: here it is estimated on the training set (80%) and checked once on the test set (20%).
Deep dive

Separation. If a value of x separates the 0s from the 1s perfectly, the likelihood keeps rising as b1 grows without limit: the maximum does not exist and the standard errors explode. Software stops with a warning; penalized (ridge) logistic regression or Firth's correction give finite estimates.

Marginal effects. The effect of x on the probability is ∂p/∂x = b1 p (1 − p): largest at p = 0.5 (b1 / 4), small in the tails.

Software defaults. statsmodels Logit estimates without a penalty (as here). scikit-learn's LogisticRegression adds a ridge penalty by default (C = 1), which shrinks the coefficients a little.

References: Cox (1958), "The regression analysis of binary sequences", JRSS B 20(2); Hosmer, Lemeshow and Sturdivant (2013), Applied Logistic Regression, 3rd ed.; Estrella and Mishkin (1998), "Predicting U.S. recessions: financial variables as leading indicators", Review of Economics and Statistics 80(1).

From the simulation to real data

  • Bank failures and credit risk: early-warning models use several financial ratios (capital, asset quality, earnings, liquidity). The logit is the classic benchmark against which machine learning models (SVM, trees, neural networks) are compared.
  • Recession probabilities: the term spread of the yield curve is one of the best single predictors of a US recession within a year (Estrella and Mishkin, 1998); central banks publish such probit and logit probabilities.
  • Credit scoring: scorecards are often logits, because the coefficients and odds ratios are easy to explain to supervisors.
  • With many inputs, check for separation, and remember that a significant coefficient does not guarantee good predictions on new data: always look at the test set too.
