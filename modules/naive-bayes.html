What this module shows

Naive Bayes classifies with Bayes' rule: start from how common each class is (the prior), multiply by how likely the observed inputs are in that class (the likelihoods), and compare.

It is "naive" because it treats the inputs as independent within each class, so the likelihood of all inputs is simply the product of one bell curve per input. Often wrong, yet the classifications are often good.

Bayes' rule in words

Before looking at a bank's ratios, how likely is it to fail? About 30% of the banks in the training set failed: that is the prior. Then we look at the evidence: how typical are this bank's ratios for failed banks, and for healthy banks? Those are the likelihoods. Bayes' rule combines them:

posterior ∝ prior × likelihood

For each class, multiply the prior by the likelihoods, then divide by the sum over the classes so that the posteriors add up to 1. The class with the largest posterior wins.

Bayes' rule with the naive assumption
P(c \mid x_1, x_2) = \frac{P(c)\; p(x_1 \mid c)\; p(x_2 \mid c)}{\sum_{c'} P(c')\; p(x_1 \mid c')\; p(x_2 \mid c')}
p(x_j \mid c) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{jc}^2}}\, \exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{(x_j - \mu_{jc})^2}{2\sigma_{jc}^2}\Bigr) \qquad \text{(Gaussian naive Bayes)}

μjc and σ²jc: the mean and the variance of input j among the training observations of class c.

Why "naive"?

The correct likelihood of both inputs together would need their joint distribution, including how they move together. Naive Bayes assumes the inputs are independent within each class, so it simply multiplies one bell curve per input. On the chart this shows as ellipses that are never tilted. When the inputs are related (profit margin and return on assets), the same evidence is counted twice and the probabilities become too extreme, too close to 0 or 1.

Why it still works

To classify we only need the largest posterior, not its exact value. Even when the probabilities are too extreme, the ranking of the classes is often right, so the accuracy stays close to that of better models such as the logit. Naive Bayes is also very fast, needs little data and copes with many inputs: the classic spam filter is a naive Bayes over thousands of words.

The priors

With priors from the training set, a rare class needs strong evidence to win. With equal priors every class starts at the same level, so the rare class (failed banks) is predicted more often: more failures found, more false alarms.

Deep dive

Discriminative and generative. The logit models P(class | x) directly (discriminative). Naive Bayes models how the inputs are generated in each class, p(x | class), and the priors (generative). With few observations naive Bayes often wins; with many, the logit catches up (Ng and Jordan, 2002).

Variance floor. As in scikit-learn's GaussianNB, a tiny amount (10−9 × the largest variance of the inputs) is added to each variance, to avoid dividing by zero.

Categorical inputs. For words or categories, the likelihood is a share in the training data. A category never seen in a class would give a likelihood of 0 and wipe out all other evidence; Laplace smoothing (add 1 to every count) prevents this.

References: Ng and Jordan (2002), "On discriminative vs. generative classifiers: a comparison of logistic regression and naive Bayes", NIPS 14; Domingos and Pazzani (1997), "On the optimality of the simple Bayesian classifier under zero-one loss", Machine Learning 29; Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman (2009), The Elements of Statistical Learning, §6.6.3.

From the simulation to real data

  • Text: classifying news, central-bank statements or customer complaints by their words (multinomial naive Bayes); spam filters.
  • A quick baseline: naive Bayes trains in an instant; if a complex model cannot beat it, the complex model is not worth it.
  • Do not trust its probabilities when the inputs are related: use it to rank or classify, or calibrate the probabilities first (see the ROC, PR & Calibration module).
  • Set the priors deliberately: the share of failed banks in a sample can differ from the share in the population.
