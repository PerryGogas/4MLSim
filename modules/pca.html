What this module shows

Many economic variables move together: the interest rates at 8 maturities, dozens of business-cycle indicators. Principal component analysis (PCA) finds a few new variables, the principal components, that capture most of their variation.

The first component is the direction along which the data vary the most; the second, at a right angle to it, the next most; and so on. Keep the first few, and you have summarized many variables with little loss.

How to use it

  1. Two indicators: turn your own direction and find the one with the largest variance. That is the first component.
  2. Yield curve: read the scree plot and the loadings (level, slope, curvature).
  3. Rebuild a month's yield curve from 1, 2 or 3 components.

Many variables, a few factors

When variables are strongly correlated, much of their information is shared. PCA rotates the data to new axes, the principal components:

  • Component 1 is the direction (a weighted combination of the inputs) along which the data have the largest variance.
  • Component 2 is at a right angle to component 1 and has the largest remaining variance; and so on. The components are uncorrelated.
  • There are as many components as inputs, but usually the first few carry most of the variance: keep those, drop the rest. This is dimension reduction.

Reading the results

  • Explained variance (the scree plot): the share of the total variance each component captures. Keep enough components for, say, 90% or 95%, or stop where the bars flatten.
  • Loadings: the weights of each input in a component. They tell what the component means.
  • Scores: the values of the components for each observation: the new, fewer variables.

The yield curve: level, slope, curvature

For interest rates at many maturities, three components explain almost all the movements, and their loadings have a clear meaning: level (all rates move together), slope (short and long rates move in opposite directions) and curvature (medium maturities move against both ends). Bond portfolio managers measure and hedge their risk with these three factors.

Standardize?

PCA looks for variance, so an input measured in large units dominates. When the inputs have different units (GDP in billions, unemployment in %), standardize first: this is PCA on the correlation matrix. When they share the same unit and scale (interest rates in %), PCA on the covariance matrix is common.

Eigenvectors of the covariance matrix
\Sigma = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_i (x_i - \bar x)(x_i - \bar x)^\top, \qquad \Sigma\, v_k = \lambda_k\, v_k, \quad \lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots
\text{scores: } z_{ik} = v_k^\top (x_i - \bar x), \qquad \text{explained share: } \frac{\lambda_k}{\sum_j \lambda_j}, \qquad \text{rebuilt: } \hat x_i = \bar x + \sum_{k=1}^{m} z_{ik}\, v_k

The variance of the data along any unit direction u is uTΣu; it is largest, equal to λ1, at u = v1. That is what the "variance by direction" chart shows.

Deep dive

Best low-dimensional approximation. Among all m-dimensional subspaces, the span of the first m components gives the smallest sum of squared reconstruction errors (Eckart-Young). In practice PCA is computed with the singular value decomposition of the centered data.

Unsupervised. PCA ignores any target variable: a direction with small variance can still be the one that predicts. Principal component regression and diffusion-index forecasting (factor models with many predictors) use the first components as regressors.

Signs. An eigenvector and its negative are equally valid; software fixes the sign by a convention (here, as scikit-learn, the largest loading is positive).
