What this module shows

A polynomial of degree d can bend d − 1 times. Degree 1 is a straight line; degree 2 adds one curve (a wage profile that rises and then flattens); higher degrees can follow almost any pattern.

More flexibility always fits the training data better. But past some point the curve starts to follow the random noise of this particular sample. It then predicts new data worse: this is overfitting. Too little flexibility is underfitting.

How to use it

  1. Move the degree slider, or press Sweep degrees.
  2. Watch the training error (blue) always fall, while the test error (orange) falls and then rises.
  3. Compare the degree chosen by the test set with the ones chosen by adjusted R², AIC and BIC.
  4. Click any ? for help, then try the Challenges.

Flexibility: how many shapes can a model take?

A polynomial regression is still a linear regression: the X variables are simply x, x², x³, and so on. Labour economists use exactly this when they put experience and experience squared in a wage equation. Each extra power adds one coefficient and lets the curve bend once more. With as many coefficients as observations the curve can pass through every single point.

Polynomial regression of degree d
y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \beta_2 x_i^2 + \dots + \beta_d x_i^d + \varepsilon_i, \qquad k = d+1 \text{ coefficients, estimated by OLS}

Training error always falls; test error does not

Adding a power can never make the fit to the training data worse: OLS can always set the new coefficient to zero. So the training error keeps falling as the degree rises. The test error measures something else: how close the curve is to new observations. At first it falls too, because the curve captures the real pattern. Then it rises, because the extra bends follow the noise of the training sample, which does not repeat. The result is the typical U-shape of the test error.

  • Underfitting (degree too low): the model misses the pattern. Training and test errors are both high and close to each other.
  • Overfitting (degree too high): the training error is small, the test error is large, and the gap between them grows.
  • No model can beat the noise: even the true curve has a test RMSE close to the noise standard deviation σ.
Training and test error
\text{RMSE}_{\text{train}} = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{n_{tr}}\textstyle\sum_{i\in tr}(y_i-\hat f(x_i))^2}, \qquad \text{RMSE}_{\text{test}} = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{n_{te}}\textstyle\sum_{i\in te}(y_i-\hat f(x_i))^2}

What helps against overfitting

  • More data: with more observations the noise averages out and higher degrees become safe. Try 200 observations.
  • Less noise: the noisier the data, the sooner extra flexibility hurts.
  • Choosing the degree honestly: with a test set or cross-validation (machine learning), or with penalized criteria such as adjusted R², AIC and BIC (econometrics).
  • Penalizing large coefficients: see the Regularization module. Look at the coefficient chart: overfitted polynomials have huge coefficients of alternating sign that cancel each other out.

Econometric criteria

Econometrics chooses among nested models with criteria that reward fit but charge for each extra coefficient. Adjusted R² charges the least, AIC more (and its small-sample version AICc more still when k is large relative to n), and BIC usually the most (its charge grows with the sample size), so BIC tends to choose the smallest model. None of them needs a test set, which matters when data are scarce, but all of them rely on the model's assumptions.

Adjusted R², AIC and BIC (Gaussian errors)
\bar R^2 = 1-\frac{\text{SSR}/(n-k)}{\text{SST}/(n-1)}, \quad \text{AIC} = n\ln\tfrac{\text{SSR}}{n} + 2k + c, \quad \text{BIC} = n\ln\tfrac{\text{SSR}}{n} + k\ln n + c
\text{AICc} = \text{AIC} + \frac{2k(k+1)}{n-k-1}

c = n(ln 2π + 1) is the same for every degree, so it does not change the choice. AICc is the small-sample correction (Hurvich and Tsai, 1989), used in the table because n/k is small here.

Extrapolation

Polynomials are especially dangerous outside the range of the data: the highest power dominates and the curve shoots off. Tick Show beyond the data to see it. A wage equation with experience to the sixth power may fit 0 to 40 years well and predict absurd wages for 45 years.

Deep dive

Optimism of the training error. For OLS with k coefficients and homoskedastic noise of variance σ², the expected training MSE is \sigma^2 (n-k)/n, while the expected MSE on new responses at the same x values is \sigma^2 (n+k)/n. The gap 2\sigma^2 k/n is the optimism that AIC estimates; it grows with k and shrinks with n (Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman, 2009, §7.4).

Numerical stability. The columns x, x², x³, ... are almost collinear (the Vandermonde matrix is badly conditioned), so naive normal equations lose all precision at high degree. 4MLSim maps x to [−1, 1] and uses the orthogonal Legendre polynomials with a QR decomposition; the fitted curve is identical, only the computation is stable. The coefficient chart converts the result back to powers of the scaled x.

Criteria in small samples. AIC and BIC are derived from large-sample approximations. When k is close to n, the log of the residual variance falls so fast that both can favour almost interpolating models; AICc corrects AIC for this (Hurvich and Tsai, 1989). In practice the candidate set is kept small relative to n; 4MLSim uses at least 5 observations per coefficient.

Runge's phenomenon. High-degree polynomials fitted to points spread over an interval oscillate strongly near its ends, even when the true function is smooth. This is why splines (piecewise low-degree polynomials) are preferred in practice.

References: Bishop (2006), Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning, §1.1; James, Witten, Hastie and Tibshirani (2021), An Introduction to Statistical Learning, 2nd ed., ch. 2 and 7; Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman (2009), The Elements of Statistical Learning, ch. 7; Wooldridge, Introductory Econometrics, ch. 6 (quadratic terms and adjusted R²).

From the simulation to real data

  • In wage equations, a quadratic in experience is the standard; higher powers (Murphy and Welch, 1990, used a quartic) should be justified by a clear gain in out-of-sample fit or by information criteria.
  • Never choose the degree, or any other setting, by looking at the test set repeatedly: then the test set is no longer new data. Use cross-validation on the training data, and keep the test set for the final check (see the Cross-Validation module).
  • For time series, the "test set" must be the most recent period: a polynomial trend that fits the past well can be badly wrong at the end of the sample.
