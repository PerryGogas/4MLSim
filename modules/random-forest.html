What this module shows

One decision tree is simple, but unstable and often inaccurate. A random forest grows many trees, each on a different bootstrap sample of the training set (a random selection with replacement) and with a random input tried at each split.

For a new observation every tree votes; the majority decides, "democratically". In regression the forest averages the trees' forecasts. Many noisy trees together give one stable model.

Why many trees?

Decision trees are simple to build, use and interpret, but in practice they are inaccurate and unstable: a small change in the training data can give a very different tree. A random forest grows many different trees and lets them vote. Their individual mistakes partly cancel out.

Bootstrap samples

Suppose the training set has n observations. A bootstrap sample is a new sample of size n, drawn at random with replacement: some observations are drawn two or three times, others not at all. On average about 63% of the observations appear in a bootstrap sample; the other 37% are out of bag for that tree. Each tree is grown on its own bootstrap sample, so the trees differ.

Share of observations left out of a bootstrap sample
P(\text{observation } i \text{ not drawn}) = \Bigl(1 - \frac{1}{n}\Bigr)^{n} \;\longrightarrow\; e^{-1} \approx 0.37

A random input at each split

To make the trees even more different, each split may only choose among a random subset of the inputs (here 1 of the 2). With all inputs at every split the method is called bagging (bootstrap aggregating): the trees then often ask the same strong question first and stay similar.

Voting and averaging

For any new data, we run it through every tree and record each tree's decision, e.g. "failed" or "healthy"; random forests decide democratically, by voting. The share of the votes is the forest's probability. In regression the forecast is the average of the trees' forecasts (the electricity demand example of the lecture).

Free checks: out-of-bag error and importance

  • Out-of-bag (OOB) error: each training observation is predicted only by the trees that did not see it (about a third of the trees). This gives an honest estimate of the error without a separate validation set.
  • Importance: how much each input reduced the impurity over all splits of all trees (normalized to add up to 100%).
Deep dive

Why decorrelation matters. The average of B trees, each with variance σ² and pairwise correlation ρ, has variance ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ²/B. More trees remove the second term, but not the first: that is why the forest also makes the trees less correlated, with random inputs at each split.

Votes or averaged probabilities. Here each tree votes for the majority class of its leaf, as in the lecture. scikit-learn averages the class shares of the leaves instead; the two rarely disagree.

No overfitting from more trees. Adding trees does not make the forest overfit; the error levels off. Deep individual trees can still make the forest follow noise; limit the depth or the leaf size, chosen by cross-validation or the OOB error.

References: Breiman (1996), "Bagging predictors", Machine Learning 24; Breiman (2001), "Random forests", Machine Learning 45; Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman (2009), The Elements of Statistical Learning, ch. 15.

From the simulation to real data

  • Random forests are a strong default for tabular data: credit risk, bank failures, demand forecasting, fraud. They need little tuning and no scaling.
  • Use a few hundred trees; tune the depth (or minimum leaf size) and the number of inputs per split with the OOB error or cross-validation.
  • Impurity importance favours inputs with many distinct values; check it with permutation importance (see the Explainability module).
