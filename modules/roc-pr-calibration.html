What this module shows

A threshold T turns probabilities into classes, and every T gives a different confusion matrix. The ROC curve and the precision-recall curve show all thresholds at once; their areas (AUC, average precision) score the model without choosing T.

The costs of the two errors then choose T. Finally, calibration asks whether the probabilities themselves can be trusted: do 30% of the banks with probability 0.3 fail?

How to use it

  1. Move T and follow the diamonds along the ROC and precision-recall curves.
  2. Make failures rare: the ROC curve hardly moves, the precision-recall curve drops.
  3. Enter the cost of a missed failure and of a false alarm, and use the lowest-cost T.
  4. Compare the calibration of the logit, naive Bayes and KNN.

The ROC curve

The ROC curve (receiver operating characteristic) is a fundamental graphical tool to evaluate binary classifiers. It shows the trade-off between the true positive rate (sensitivity) and the false positive rate (false alarms, 1 − specificity) across all thresholds. It starts at (0, 0) with T = 1 (nothing is called failed) and ends at (1, 1) with T = 0 (everything is called failed); each point in between is one threshold. This lets us pick the point that is optimal for the problem at hand.

AUC, the area under the ROC curve: 1.0 = perfect, around 0.8 = good, 0.5 = random (the diagonal). Intuitively: if we pick at random one failed bank and one healthy bank, AUC is the probability that the model gives the failed bank the higher probability.

The precision-recall curve

The precision-recall (PR) curve plots precision (of the banks called failed, how many failed?) against recall (sensitivity) for every threshold. A useless model has a precision equal to the share of failed banks, so the baseline is low when failures are rare. Its area is the average precision (AP).

Why both? Sensitivity and false alarms are shares within each actual class, so the ROC curve barely changes when failures become rare. Precision mixes the two classes: with rare failures, even a few percent of false alarms outnumber the true alarms, and the PR curve drops. When the 1s are rare and the alarms matter, read the PR curve.

Choosing T with the costs of the errors

A misclassification has a cost, and the costs are usually not symmetric: a missed bank failure (FN) costs the deposit insurance far more than an extra inspection after a false alarm (FP). With a cost per FN and per FP, the total cost of a threshold is costFN × FN + costFP × FP. We choose the T with the lowest total cost on the training set, and check it once on the test set. The more expensive a missed failure, the lower the best T.

Total cost and the best threshold
\text{Cost}(T) = c_{FN}\, FN(T) + c_{FP}\, FP(T), \qquad T^* = \arg\min_T \text{Cost}(T)

If the probabilities are well calibrated, the best threshold is close to T* = cFP / (cFP + cFN): with cFN = 5 and cFP = 1, T* ≈ 1/6 ≈ 0.17.

Calibration

A model can rank the banks well (high AUC) and still give probabilities that are too high or too low. A model is calibrated if, among the banks it gives a probability of about 0.3, about 30% fail. The reliability diagram groups the test banks into 10 bins by predicted probability and plots the share that actually failed: a calibrated model lies on the diagonal. The Brier score, the average of (probability − outcome)², measures accuracy and calibration together (0 = perfect).

  • The logit is usually well calibrated: maximum likelihood fits the probabilities themselves.
  • Naive Bayes tends to be too extreme when the inputs are related (see its module).
  • KNN gives coarse probabilities (shares of 15 votes) and can be off in thin regions.
Brier score
\text{Brier} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (p_i - y_i)^2
Deep dive

AUC and the Mann-Whitney statistic. AUC equals the Mann-Whitney U statistic divided by (number of 1s × number of 0s): the share of all (failed, healthy) pairs ranked correctly, with ties counted as half.

Average precision. AP = Σ (Rn − Rn−1) Pn over the thresholds, as in scikit-learn; it does not interpolate between points, which would be too optimistic.

Recalibration. Platt scaling (a logit on the model's score) or isotonic regression, fitted on validation data, repair poor calibration without changing the ranking (AUC stays the same).

References: Fawcett (2006), "An introduction to ROC analysis", Pattern Recognition Letters 27(8); Saito and Rehmsmeier (2015), "The precision-recall plot is more informative than the ROC plot when evaluating binary classifiers on imbalanced datasets", PLoS ONE 10(3); Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana (2005), "Predicting good probabilities with supervised learning", ICML; Elkan (2001), "The foundations of cost-sensitive learning", IJCAI.

From the simulation to real data

  • Report AUC, and with rare events (failures, fraud, crises) also the precision-recall curve and AP: an AUC of 0.95 can come with a precision of 20%.
  • Agree on the costs of the two errors with the decision maker, then choose T on the training (or validation) data, never on the test set.
  • If the probabilities are used as probabilities (pricing loans, provisions, early-warning dashboards), check the reliability diagram and the Brier score, and recalibrate when needed.
  • In the lecture example, choosing between points A, B and C of the ROC curve is exactly this cost decision.
