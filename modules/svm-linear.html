What this module shows

A support vector machine (SVM) finds the "best" separator between two classes. There are infinitely many lines that separate failed from healthy banks; the SVM chooses the one with the widest margin, the widest empty street between the classes.

The observations on the edges of the street (or inside it) are the support vectors: only they decide where the line goes. Since perfect separation never occurs in real data, the price of errors C decides how many observations may sit inside the street or on the wrong side.

Classification problems for an SVM

Up or down of an asset's price, growth or recession, failed or healthy bank, fraud or legitimate card payment: two classes, and a few inputs that describe each case.

The best separator

When two classes can be separated by a straight line, there is an infinite number of lines that do it. The SVM picks the one that stays as far as possible from both classes: the middle of the widest street that has no observations in it. That street is the margin; its edges are the dashed lines. A wide margin leaves room for new observations that differ a little from the training data.

With n inputs the separator is a hyperplane with n − 1 dimensions: a point for 1 input, a line for 2, a plane for 3, and so on.

Support vectors

The observations on the edges of the street, or inside it, hold the street in place: these are the support vectors. All the other observations could be moved or deleted (as long as they stay outside the street) and the line would not change. That is why the method is called a support vector machine.

Soft margin: the price of errors C

Perfect separation never occurs in real data (the credit card example of the lecture: legitimate and fraudulent payments overlap). The SVM then allows some observations inside the street or on the wrong side, and charges a price C for each unit of violation.

  • Small C: errors are cheap. A wide street with many support vectors; a smooth, cautious separator.
  • Large C: errors are expensive. A narrow street that tries to classify every training observation correctly; it can overfit.
  • C is chosen by cross-validation on the training set; the test set is used once.
The optimization problem
f(x) = w^\top x + b, \qquad \text{margin width} = \frac{2}{\lVert w \rVert}
\min_{w,\,b}\; \frac{1}{2}\lVert w \rVert^2 + C \sum_i \max\bigl(0,\; 1 - y_i f(x_i)\bigr), \qquad y_i \in \{-1, +1\}

The first term widens the street; the second (the hinge loss) charges every observation inside the street or on the wrong side. A point is classified by the sign of f(x).

Deep dive

The dual. The solution can be written as w = Σ αi yi xi, with 0 ≤ αi ≤ C. Only the support vectors have αi > 0: those with αi < C lie exactly on the margin, those with αi = C inside it or on the wrong side. The dual depends on the data only through dot products xi⊤xj, which opens the door to the kernel trick (next module).

Computation. The dual is solved here with sequential minimal optimization (SMO), as in LIBSVM and scikit-learn's SVC, on standardized inputs; the decision values match scikit-learn.

Scaling matters. The margin is measured in the units of the inputs, so they must be standardized first.

References: Cortes and Vapnik (1995), "Support-vector networks", Machine Learning 20; Platt (1998), "Sequential minimal optimization", Microsoft Research TR-98-14; Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman (2009), The Elements of Statistical Learning, ch. 12.

From the simulation to real data

  • Bank failures: in the lecture example a linear SVM on two ratios (Tier 1 capital and net interest expense over net interest income) misclassified only 10 of 1,279 US banks.
  • Standardize the inputs; choose C by cross-validation on the training set; report the test results once.
  • With many inputs a linear SVM is fast and often enough; when the classes are separated by a curve, use a kernel (next module).
