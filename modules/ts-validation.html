What this module shows

A forecaster predicts monthly inflation with a machine learning model. Every month she stands at now: she knows the months up to now and nothing after. How do we check such a model on past data, without letting it see the future?

  • Training set: the first 80% of the months. Forecasts of these months give the validation error, used to choose the method.
  • Test (hold-out) set: the last 20% of the months. Their forecasts give the test error, reported once at the end.

Three methods, in this order: 1. time-series split (blocks of months, like the usual cross-validation), 2. expanding window, 3. rolling window.

How to use it

  1. Start with method 1 and press Next (or Play) until the end.
  2. Then choose method 2, walk to the end; then method 3.
  3. After all three, the comparison table shows which method is chosen and its test error.
  4. Switch the regime change on and off and compare again. At the bottom: why shuffled k-fold is wrong for a time series.

Why time series need their own validation

In ordinary cross-validation the observations can be shuffled into folds: their order does not matter. In a time series the order is the whole point. A forecaster knows the months up to today and nothing after. A validation that lets the model see later months measures a skill the forecaster will never have.

The words we use

  • Now: the month in which the forecast is made, the last month we know.
  • Input month: the month whose data we use to forecast.
  • Target month: the month we forecast, h months after the input month.
  • Horizon h: how many months ahead we forecast.

The model

inflation in the target month = f(latest inflation values),   \pi_{t+h} = f(\pi_t, \pi_{t-1}, \ldots, \pi_{t-p})

f is learned from training examples (input month, target month): for example with h = 3, the examples (1, 4), (2, 5), (3, 6), and so on. f can be any machine learning model: a linear model, a random forest, a neural network. In this simulation it is a simple linear one, so that the effect of the evaluation methods is easy to see; the same evaluation applies to any ML model.

Static forecasts. Each forecast uses the actual inflation of its input month: inflation is published every month, so when month 60 is forecast with h = 1, the actual value of month 59 is known. A dynamic forecast would instead feed the model's own earlier forecasts back in as inputs, as if no new data arrived after the end of training. This module uses static forecasts.

The gap. With h = 3, a model trained on months up to 50 makes its first forecast for month 53. Months 51 and 52, h − 1 = 2 months, lie in between: they are not yet known when the forecast is made. In the windows chart this empty space is labelled gap.

For training we use only the examples whose target month is already known. With h = 3 and now = month 50, the last training example is (47, 50). Why not (48, 51)? When we evaluate on past data, our data file already contains months 51, 52, 53 and so on, so we could train with (48, 51). But a forecaster at month 50 does not know month 51 yet. Using it would be leakage, and the error would look smaller than in real forecasting.

Method 1: time-series split

It looks like the usual k-fold cross-validation, with one rule added: train only on months before the validation block. With 40 training months and blocks of 10:

  1. Round 1: train on months 1-10, validate on months 11-20.
  2. Round 2: train on months 1-20, validate on months 21-30.
  3. Round 3: train on months 1-30, validate on months 31-40.

The validation error is computed over all validated months. The model is trained once per round. (In scikit-learn: TimeSeriesSplit.)

Method 2: expanding window

The same idea with blocks of 1 month: the model is re-trained every month on all months from 1 to now, and forecasts the next target month. The training window grows by one month each time. Econometricians call this the recursive scheme.

Method 3: rolling window

Like the expanding window, but the model is trained only on the last W months (with W = 24: now = month 50 trains on months 27-50). Old months drop out. Also called a sliding window.

  • When the series behaves the same way all the time, more data is better: the expanding window usually wins.
  • After a regime change (a new monetary policy, a crisis, an energy shock), old months describe a world that no longer exists. The rolling window forgets them after W months; the expanding window keeps them forever.

Choosing and testing

  • Each method gets a validation error on the training months. The method with the smallest validation error is chosen.
  • The test is done once, as in the Cross-Validation module: the final model is trained on the whole training set (months 1-96 at the defaults; rolling window: its last W months, 73-96) and forecasts all test months, 97-120, without re-training. Its test error is reported once.
  • In practice forecasters often keep re-training their model as new months arrive. That is a different exercise from the final test, which checks once the model we chose.

Why not shuffled k-fold?

Shuffled folds put random months in the validation fold. To validate month 50, the model is trained on the other folds, which include months 51, 52, ... after it: the future. With a regime change, the model then learns the new regime from months it is not supposed to know, and its error looks too small. Flexible ML models can cheat even more, by copying neighbouring months (see the Data Leakage module).

The model in this simulation
f(\pi_t) = a + b\,\pi_t, \qquad (\hat a, \hat b) = \arg\min_{a,b} \sum_{s=L}^{\,\text{now}-h} \bigl(\pi_{s+h} - a - b\,\pi_s\bigr)^2

Expanding window and time-series split: L = 1. Rolling window of length W: L = \text{now} - W + 1. The upper limit now − h keeps only examples whose target month is known.

\text{validation error} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_V}\sum \bigl(\pi_{\text{target}} - \hat\pi_{\text{target}}\bigr)^2}\quad\text{(RMSE over the forecasts of the training months)}
Deep dive

When is k-fold acceptable? Bergmeir, Hyndman and Koo (2018) show that for a purely autoregressive model with uncorrelated errors on a stable series, standard k-fold cross-validation is valid. Problems arise with flexible models that can copy neighbouring observations, with correlated errors, and with series that change over time.

Breaks and window choice. Pesaran and Timmermann (2007) study how to choose the estimation window when there are structural breaks: post-break windows reduce bias but increase variance.

Real-time data. Macroeconomic series are revised. An honest evaluation uses, at each month, the data published at that time (Croushore and Stark, 2001), not today's revised series.

References: Tashman (2000), "Out-of-sample tests of forecasting accuracy: an analysis and review", International Journal of Forecasting 16(4); Bergmeir, Hyndman and Koo (2018), "A note on the validity of cross-validation for evaluating autoregressive time series prediction", Computational Statistics & Data Analysis 120; Pesaran and Timmermann (2007), "Selection of estimation window in the presence of breaks", Journal of Econometrics 137(1); Croushore and Stark (2001), "A real-time data set for macroeconomists", Journal of Econometrics 105(1); Hyndman and Athanasopoulos, Forecasting: Principles and Practice, 3rd ed., §5.10; scikit-learn documentation, TimeSeriesSplit.

From the simulation to real data

  • Set the horizon h to the decision: a central bank cares about inflation 12 to 24 months ahead, a treasury desk about next month.
  • Keep the last 20% of the months as the test (hold-out) set. Choose models, methods and window lengths with the validation error on the earlier months only.
  • Look at the errors over time: are they large around crises or policy changes? A rolling window may be needed.
  • For macroeconomic series, use the data published at each date (real-time data), not today's revised series.
