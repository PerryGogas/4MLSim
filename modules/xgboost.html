What this module shows

XGBoost (extreme gradient boosting) is essentially a decision "enhancer": like gradient boosting it adds small trees one after another, each correcting the errors left. It starts with a simple prediction (a probability of 0.5 for everyone, or the average) and builds each tree with exact formulas from the gradients g and curvatures h of the errors.

Two penalties keep the trees small: λ shrinks the leaf values and γ removes splits that gain too little. It is one of the most efficient algorithms and almost always among the best on tabular data.

How to use it

  1. Press Reset, then Step, and read the tree of each round: G, H and the gain of every split.
  2. Follow the numbers of the first split in the box under the tree.
  3. Raise λ and γ and watch the trees shrink and the test result change.
  4. Compare training and test results by round.

The algorithm

  1. Initialization: start with a simple prediction, e.g. a probability of 0.5 for all observations (classification) or the average (regression).
  2. For every observation compute the gradient g (the direction and size of its error: forecast − actual, or probability − actual) and the curvature h (1 for squared error; p(1 − p) for classification).
  3. Grow a small tree. For every possible split, sum g and h on each side (GL, HL, GR, HR) and compute the gain; keep the split with the largest gain.
  4. Each leaf gets the weight w = −G / (H + λ): the best correction for the observations in it.
  5. Remove (prune) splits whose gain is below γ; add the tree multiplied by the learning rate η; repeat.
Leaf weight and split gain (as in the xgboost library)
w^* = -\frac{G}{H + \lambda}, \qquad \text{Gain} = \frac{G_L^2}{H_L + \lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R + \lambda} - \frac{(G_L + G_R)^2}{H_L + H_R + \lambda}
\text{squared error: } g_i = \hat y_i - y_i,\ h_i = 1; \qquad \text{classification: } g_i = p_i - y_i,\ h_i = p_i (1 - p_i)

A split is kept if its gain is above γ (the library prunes bottom-up after growing the tree). A leaf must also have H ≥ the minimum child weight.

Comparison with random forests

  • Random forest: many trees trained in parallel and independently; the final decision is a vote (or average). Deep trees, little tuning.
  • XGBoost: trees trained in sequence, each correcting the previous ones; small trees, more settings to tune, usually higher accuracy. In the lecture example of US GDP forecasting, XGBoost also ranks the top 15 inputs by importance.

The settings

  • η (learning rate): the share of each tree that is added. Smaller: more rounds, better generalization.
  • λ: pulls the leaf weights towards 0 (like ridge regression). Larger λ: smaller corrections, smoother model.
  • γ: the minimum gain for a split to stay. Larger γ: fewer, more useful splits.
  • Depth and minimum child weight: limit the size of each tree.
Deep dive

Second-order approximation. XGBoost approximates the loss of each observation around the current forecast by a parabola (Taylor expansion with g and h). The objective of a tree with leaf weights w becomes Σleaves [G w + ½ (H + λ) w²] + γ T, which is minimized by w* = −G/(H + λ) and gives the gain formula (the paper writes it with a factor ½ and subtracts γ; the library compares the gain without the ½ to γ, as here).

Engineering. The library adds sparsity-aware splits for missing values, histogram and approximate split finding for big data, column subsampling and parallel computing; results here match xgboost's exact method.

References: Chen and Guestrin (2016), "XGBoost: a scalable tree boosting system", KDD; Friedman (2001), "Greedy function approximation: a gradient boosting machine", Annals of Statistics 29(5).

From the simulation to real data

  • XGBoost (and its relatives LightGBM and CatBoost) win most competitions on tabular data: credit scoring, fraud, demand and GDP forecasting.
  • Typical tuning: η 0.01 to 0.1 with early stopping on validation data, depth 3 to 8, λ and γ by cross-validation, plus row and column subsampling.
  • Use the library's importance measures, or better SHAP values, to explain the predictions (see the Explainability module).
